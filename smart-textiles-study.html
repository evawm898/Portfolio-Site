    01Electrons and the Push

    Everything on the note runs on one idea: a voltage pushes charge round a closed loop, and whatever is in the way decides how much gets through. Three numbers carry it: voltage is the push (volts), current is the flow (amps), resistance is the fight (ohms).

    Two things trip everyone up, and both stop being confusing once you’ve watched them. The electrons themselves crawl, while the push arrives almost instantly. And the current arrow on every diagram points the opposite way to the electrons.

    Watch

    The units, as a cheat sheet
    V · volt
    The push. How hard charge is driven. A coin cell is 3 V, a phone battery 3.7 V, USB 5 V.
    I · amp
    The flow. How much charge passes a point per second. An LED wants about 0.02 A; a heated glove about 1 A.
    R · ohm
    The fight. How hard the path resists. A metre of conductive thread is anywhere from about 1 to 1,000 Ω.
    P · watt
    The work. Power turned into heat or light each second: P = V × I.
    Q · coulomb
    An amount of charge, the thing a capacitor stores. You’ll meet it again in chapter 04.

    02Why a Yarn Resists

    A yarn’s resistance comes from three things: what it’s made of (resistivity, ρ), how long it is, and how much conductor is in its cross-section. That’s R = ρL/A. Textiles add a fourth that a wire doesn’t have: contacts, every place the current hops from filament to filament or loop to loop.

    If you think in textiles: ρ is like fibre density, a property of the material. A yarn’s Ω/m is like its tex, because it bakes in how much material is in the cross-section. Compare materials by ρ, compare yarns by Ω/m.

    03Choosing the Metal

    Conductivity is the first number people quote and rarely the one that decides. In a garment the metal also has to survive sweat and washing, sit against skin, flex millions of times, and be joined to a board. Each metal wins a different subset of those.

    Rule of thumb, to argue with once you’ve read the cards: silver for skin, copper (always dressed) for wiring, gold where things touch, stainless where it has to survive, carbon where it has to sense.

    04Resistive vs Capacitive

    Both kinds of sensor turn a press, a stretch or a touch into an electrical number, by different routes. A resistive sensor lets current through, and the press changes how easily it passes. A capacitive sensor has an insulator in the middle that current can’t cross. It stores charge on either side, and the press changes how much it can store.

    That one difference decides almost everything else: whether it needs force or only presence, what sweat does to it, whether it drifts, and how the chip reads it.

    05Stack-Ups

    A textile sensor or heater is a stack of layers, and most failures come from a layer that’s missing or in the wrong place, more often than from the wrong material. Pick a stack to watch it come apart and go back together. Tap a layer to see what it’s for and what happens without it.

    Then rebuild them from a shuffled pile. Get the order wrong and it tells you what that mistake would do to the real thing.
