Dyeing

Color can go in at almost any point in the chain — into the polymer before there is a fiber, into loose fiber, into yarn, or into finished fabric. Where it goes in, and which dye is used, decides how the color behaves for the rest of the product’s life.

The fiber decides the dye: each fiber only takes the dye chemistry that can get into it, or hold on to it.

10.1

Where Color Enters

Pick a stage and watch the dye go in there. Everything after it inherits the color; everything before it is still undyed.

The trade is always the same: the earlier the color goes in, the deeper and steadier it is — and the earlier you have to commit to it.

Pigment goes into the melted polymer before the fiber exists, so the color is the fiber. The most fade-resistant in sunlight, and there’s no dye bath, so no dye effluent. But the color is chosen before anything else is.

10.2

Inside, On, or Into

Pigment and dye are different things. Pigment is solid particles; it has no hold on a fiber of its own, so it’s either locked inside the polymer or glued onto the surface. Dye is molecules that go into the fiber.

10.3

Why the Fiber Decides the Dye

Zoom into a fiber and it’s long chains of molecules. A dye only works on a fiber if it can get in among those chains and stay there — and each fiber offers a different way to hold on.

So each fiber has its dye class: disperse for polyester, acid for nylon, reactive for cotton.

10.4

One Bath, Two Dyes — Cross Dyeing

Because each dye only takes to its own fiber, a fabric blended from two fibers can go into one bath with two dyes and come out two colors. That’s cross dyeing.

Choose the two dyes to match instead and the same bath gives one solid color: union dyeing.

  • Disperse dye → polyester: the smooth, straight filaments running up and down
  • Acid dye → nylon: the crimped filaments running across

Reserve dyeing. Leave one dye out and that fiber comes out undyed — a two-tone, or a heather if the fibers are mixed in the yarn.

Polyester with cotton is harder: disperse dye wants a hot, acidic bath and reactive dye an alkaline one. So the two usually run in the same bath as two steps, one after the other.
