Defining Durability

— Do something to the fabric N times, then measure what’s left. How textile labs test durability, machine by machine, and why their numbers don’t convert.

01Performance over time

Durability is performance retained over time. Nearly every test has the same shape: do something to the fabric N times, then measure what’s left.

Different machines do different things to it, so their numbers aren’t interchangeable. That’s why a spec always names the test: “30,000 double rubs” means nothing without “Wyzenbeek, ASTM D4157” beside it.

In the figures below, fabric is drawn in teal, the machine in grey, abradants in tan, and the instrument’s output in orange on its own chart. Every count, curve and end point is illustrative unless a source is named: the shapes follow the methods, the values are not measurements. Each test plays once when it scrolls into view; Restart, or a click on the figure, runs it again.

02Abrasion

Rubbing, until something gives. Three machines dominate, and they rub differently, report in different units, and don’t always rank fabrics in the same order.

03Martindale

ASTM D4966 · ISO 12947

In use
Sitting, shifting, brushing against things: wear that comes from every direction.
The machine
A small disc of the sample, under load, is rubbed against a stationary wool abradant along a Lissajous figure.

The path is the point. It opens from a straight line into a widening ellipse and closes to the line at right angles, and one whole figure is 16 rubs (ISO 12947-1). Over a figure the sample is rubbed in every direction in turn, so the test doesn’t favour warp or weft. It runs until a thread breaks or a hole appears, and reports cycles.

The same machine also runs a pilling test (section 06).

04Wyzenbeek

ASTM D4157 · oscillatory cylinder

In use
Sliding in and out of the same seat, along the same line, for years.
The machine
A curved drum faced with abradant swings back and forth under a sample clamped flat across its top, under tension.

It rubs in one direction only, so warp and weft are tested as separate specimens. One forward-and-back pass is a double rub, the unit on upholstery specs.

The Association for Contract Textiles’ guidelines give thresholds in both units, side by side, with no conversion between them: 15,000 double rubs (Wyzenbeek) or 20,000 cycles (Martindale) for low-traffic upholstery, and 30,000 or 40,000 for high traffic.

05Taber

ASTM D3884 · rotary platform

In use
Scuffing and grinding: a heel on a floor mat, a bag strap on a seat.
The machine
The sample turns on a platform under two abrasive wheels, which are turned only by the sample moving beneath them.

The wheels don’t just roll: their axes miss the platform’s centre, so each one slides a little as it rolls, and that slip is what abrades. The two wheels slip in opposite senses, which wears a ring-shaped track scuffed criss-cross. Results are usually reported as mass loss, or appearance, after a set number of cycles. ASTM D3884 is published as a guide rather than a test method.

06Pilling and snagging

Pilling

Pilling is not wear-through. Fibres work loose and ball up: first a fuzz, then tangles held to the surface by a few anchoring fibres. The surface is graded by eye against reference photographs, 5 (no pilling) to 1 (very severe).

Three common ways to make it happen: Martindale pilling (ASTM D4970), the same machine as section 03; the random tumble tester (ASTM D3512), which tumbles specimens in a cork-lined chamber; and the ICI pill box (ISO 12945-1), which tumbles specimens mounted on tubes in a rotating cork-lined box.

Snagging

A snag is a loop pulled out of the fabric by something sharp, a branch or a ring. The mace test (ASTM D3939) bounces a spiked ball on a fabric-covered turning drum and grades the pulled loops against reference photographs.

07Strength: three ways to break

Three ways to pull a fabric apart, on the same layout: the specimen on the left, the instrument’s force trace on the right.

Tensile · ASTM D5034 grab · D5035 strip

Tear · ASTM D1424 Elmendorf · D2261 tongue · D5587 trapezoid

Burst · ASTM D3786 diaphragm · D3787 ball

Tensile pulls along one direction. Tear starts from a cut and asks how hard it is to keep it going: each tooth of the trace is one yarn. Burst pushes on a clamped circle, so the fabric is stretched in every direction at once.

Knits are burst-tested because pulling a knit in one direction mostly measures how far it stretches, not when it breaks. And for many fabrics tear is what actually fails in use: a small cut or snag that keeps running.

08Seams

A seam can fail two ways. Seam strength: the seam itself breaks. Seam slippage: the seam holds, but the yarns beside it slide apart and open a gap.

ASTM D1683 covers both in woven fabrics, reporting seam strength and the force at which yarns slip at the seam. For woven upholstery, slippage at the seam has its own method, ASTM D4034.

09Fatigue: recovery that fades

How far a fabric stretches and how well it comes back is on Quantifying Comfort, as a feel. Here it’s the same loop, retained: run it again and again and each loop leaves a little more permanent growth.

Elastane is what gives most stretch fabrics their recovery, and it degrades with repeated stretching, laundering, heat, sweat and especially chlorine: in one study, swimwear fabrics containing elastane lost about 65% of their breaking force after 300 hours in chlorinated water (Potočić Matković et al., 2024). That matters most for garments worn against the skin every day, where the fabric is stretched, washed and sweated in on repeat.

Cyclic tests exist for the loop itself: ASTM D4964 takes elastic fabrics through repeated loading cycles and reads tension and recovery, and ISO 20932-1 runs its strip test for an agreed number of cycles.

10Colourfastness and shrinkage

Crocking is colour rubbing off. On a crockmeter (AATCC TM8), a white test cloth on a rubbing finger is rubbed back and forth over the dyed fabric, and the stain it picks up is graded.

  • Light: exposure under a xenon-arc lamp, then the colour change is graded (AATCC TM16.3).
  • Wash: an accelerated laundering run that stands in for several home washes (AATCC TM61).
  • Perspiration: the fabric is wetted with artificial perspiration, held under pressure against undyed fabrics, and both are graded (AATCC TM15).
  • Shrinkage: dimensional change is measured between marks before and after home laundering (AATCC TM135).
