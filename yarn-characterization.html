Units & Terminology

A yarn label answers four questions: how heavy the yarn is for its length, how many filaments are in it, how many strands are twisted together, and what was done to it after spinning. Every later section on this page uses this vocabulary.

The trap is that there are two kinds of count, and they run in opposite directions.

Two families of count
Direct — mass per length

Weigh a fixed length. A heavier, thicker yarn gives a bigger number. Synthetic filament, silk and most technical yarns are sold this way.

Denier D
grams per 9,000 m
Decitex dtex
grams per 10,000 m
Tex tex
grams per 1,000 m — the SI-coherent base unit
Indirect — length per mass

Count how much length fits in a fixed weight. A heavier, thicker yarn gives a smaller number. Spun cotton and wool yarns are usually sold this way.

Cotton count Ne
840-yard hanks per pound
Metric count Nm
kilometres per kilogram

Conversions, through tex. dtex = 1.111 × D  —  tex = D ÷ 9  —  Nm = 9,000 ÷ D = 1,000 ÷ tex  —  Ne = 5,315 ÷ D = 590.5 ÷ tex  —  Ne = 0.5905 × Nm.

The 590.5 is not a fitted number: 840 yards is 768.096 m and a pound is 453.59237 g, so a yarn of Ne 1 weighs 453.59 g per 0.768 km — 590.5 g per km. Mixing up the direction is the most common spec error: “Ne 40” is finer than “Ne 20”, and “40 D” is finer than “80 D”.

Filaments, denier per filament, and how a spec is written

A filament yarn is a bundle of continuous filaments. Its count is the whole bundle; divide by the number of filaments to get denier per filament (dpf), the fineness of each one. 150 D in 48 filaments is 3.1 dpf; 150 D in 288 filaments is 0.52 dpf. Below about 1 dpf a yarn is usually called a microfiber. That is a naming threshold, not a quality grade, and the cut-off varies by region and trade body.

The common ways of writing it:

150D/48F
150 denier, 48 filaments. The same yarn in decitex is 167dtex/48f (150 × 1.111).
2/150/48
Plies / denier / filaments: two 150D/48F yarns twisted together, 300 D and 96 filaments in all.
75D/72F × 2
The same idea, with the ply count written after.
2/30s
Cotton count: two Ne 30 singles plied. Because the system is indirect, the resultant is about Ne 15 (30 ÷ 2), heavier than each single.
Nm 48/2
Metric count with the plies written after: two Nm 48 singles, about Nm 24 together.

Regions write ply and count in either order in the indirect systems (2/30s and 30/2 mean the same yarn), so the decoder below reads the smaller number as the plies. A plied yarn also weighs a few percent more than plies × count, because twisting takes up length.

Spec decoder pick one, or type your own

Reads 150D/48F, 167dtex/48f, 2/150/48, 75D/72F x2, 30s, 2/30s, Ne 30, Nm 48/2, plus POY, FDY, DTY or ATY and a fibre name. The filament diameter assumes a round, solid filament.

Process states of synthetic filament

A polyester or nylon filament yarn passes through named states between the spinneret and the fabric. The label tells you which state you are buying.

POYPartially oriented yarn

Spun fast and only partly drawn, so its molecules are only partly aligned. It stretches easily and is unstable. A feedstock for texturing, not a yarn for fabric.

FDYFully drawn yarn

Drawn to full orientation as it is spun. Flat, smooth, strong and lustrous, with no crimp.

DTYDraw-textured yarn

POY drawn and false-twist textured in one step. Each filament keeps a heat-set helical crimp: bulk, softness and some stretch.

ATYAir-textured yarn

Filament overfed into an air jet that blows loops into it. Matte and spun-like, with little stretch.

POY → FDY is drawing; POY → DTY is drawing plus false-twist texturing; ATY is made from POY or FDY. How texturing works, and what each type does to a fabric, is a later section.

Three distinctions that recur
Filament vs staple

Filament is continuous, kilometres long: smooth, lustrous, with no ends to fuzz. Staple is short fibre, millimetres to centimetres, drafted into overlap and twisted. Its ends stick out, making it soft, matte and warmer to the touch. Synthetics can be either; cotton and wool are only staple.

Singles vs plied

A single is one strand. A plied yarn twists two or more singles together, usually in the opposite direction to their own twist. Plying evens out weak spots, balances the twist’s torque and adds strength, at the cost of more processing.

S vs Z twist

Hold the yarn upright. If the fibres slope like the middle stroke of an S, it is S-twist; like the middle of a Z, Z-twist. Singles are most often Z, and the ply twist over them S. Section 03 shows both directions wrapping a core.

Sources ISO 1144 and ASTM D861 (the tex system); ASTM D2260 (conversion factors between yarn numbering systems); ISO 2060 and ASTM D1907 (linear density by the skein method); ISO 2 (designation of twist direction). Fibre densities used for the filament diameter: Morton & Hearle, Physical Properties of Textile Fibres.

Covered Yarn — Twist Direction

A covered yarn wraps a cover yarn around an elastic core. Single covered yarn (SCY) has one wrap, in either twist direction — S or Z, named for which way the diagonal of the letter runs. Double covered yarn (DCY) wraps twice: the inner layer goes on Z, then the outer layer wraps S over it, so the two torques cancel.
