Units & Terminology

A yarn label answers four questions: how heavy the yarn is for its length, how many filaments are in it, how many strands are twisted together, and what was done to it after spinning. Every later section on this page uses this vocabulary.

The trap is that there are two kinds of count, and they run in opposite directions.

Two families of count
Direct — mass per length

Weigh a fixed length. A heavier, thicker yarn gives a bigger number. Synthetic filament, silk and most technical yarns are sold this way.

Conversions, through tex. dtex = 1.111 × D  —  tex = D ÷ 9  —  Nm = 9,000 ÷ D = 1,000 ÷ tex  —  Ne = 5,315 ÷ D = 590.5 ÷ tex  —  Ne = 0.5905 × Nm.

The 590.5 is not a fitted number: 840 yards is 768.096 m and a pound is 453.59237 g, so a yarn of Ne 1 weighs 453.59 g per 0.768 km — 590.5 g per km. Mixing up the direction is the most common spec error: “Ne 40” is finer than “Ne 20”, and “40 D” is finer than “80 D”.

Filaments, denier per filament, and how a spec is written

A filament yarn is a bundle of continuous filaments. Its count is the whole bundle; divide by the number of filaments to get denier per filament (dpf), the fineness of each one. 150 D in 48 filaments is 3.1 dpf; 150 D in 288 filaments is 0.52 dpf. Below about 1 dpf a yarn is usually called a microfiber. That is a naming threshold, not a quality grade, and the cut-off varies by region and trade body.

The common ways of writing it:

150D/48F
150 denier, 48 filaments. The same yarn in decitex is 167dtex/48f (150 × 1.111).
2/150/48
Plies / denier / filaments: two 150D/48F yarns twisted together, 300 D and 96 filaments in all.
75D/72F × 2
The same idea, with the ply count written after.
2/30s
Cotton count: two Ne 30 singles plied. Because the system is indirect, the resultant is about Ne 15 (30 ÷ 2), heavier than each single.
Nm 48/2
Metric count with the plies written after: two Nm 48 singles, about Nm 24 together.

Regions write ply and count in either order in the indirect systems (2/30s and 30/2 mean the same yarn), so the decoder below reads the smaller number as the plies. A plied yarn also weighs a few percent more than plies × count, because twisting takes up length.

Spec decoder pick one, or type your own

Reads 150D/48F, 167dtex/48f, 2/150/48, 75D/72F x2, 30s, 2/30s, Ne 30, Nm 48/2, plus POY, FDY, DTY or ATY and a fibre name. The filament diameter assumes a round, solid filament.

Three more worked specs are in Textile 101 — Reading a Yarn Spec.

Process states of synthetic filament

A polyester or nylon filament yarn passes through named states between the spinneret and the fabric. The label tells you which state you are buying.

POYPartially oriented yarn

Spun fast and only partly drawn, so its molecules are only partly aligned. It stretches easily and is unstable. A feedstock for texturing, not a yarn for fabric.

FDYFully drawn yarn

Drawn to full orientation as it is spun. Flat, smooth, strong and lustrous, with no crimp.

DTYDraw-textured yarn

POY drawn and false-twist textured in one step. Each filament keeps a heat-set helical crimp: bulk, softness and some stretch.

ATYAir-textured yarn

Filament overfed into an air jet that blows loops into it. Matte and spun-like, with little stretch.

POY → FDY is drawing; POY → DTY is drawing plus false-twist texturing; ATY is made from POY or FDY. How texturing works, and what each type does to a fabric, is a later section.

Three distinctions that recur
Filament vs staple

Filament is continuous, kilometres long: smooth, lustrous, with no ends to fuzz. Staple is short fibre, millimetres to centimetres, drafted into overlap and twisted. Its ends stick out, making it soft, matte and warmer to the touch. Synthetics can be either; cotton and wool are only staple.

Singles vs plied

A single is one strand. A plied yarn twists two or more singles together, usually in the opposite direction to their own twist. Plying evens out weak spots, balances the twist’s torque and adds strength, at the cost of more processing.

S vs Z twist

Hold the yarn upright. If the fibres slope like the middle stroke of an S, it is S-twist; like the middle of a Z, Z-twist. Singles are most often Z, and the ply twist over them S. Section 03 shows both directions wrapping a core.

Sources ISO 1144 and ASTM D861 (the tex system); ASTM D2260 (conversion factors between yarn numbering systems); ISO 2060 and ASTM D1907 (linear density by the skein method); ISO 2 (designation of twist direction). Twist

Twist is the biggest single lever on how a spun yarn behaves. It decides how strong the yarn is, how hard or soft it feels, how much it shines, and whether it lies still or kinks back on itself.

Turns per length tell you how much twist was put in. The twist multiplier tells you what that twist does, and only the multiplier compares across yarn counts.

Counting twist, and why turns alone mislead

Twist is counted as turns per metre (TPM) or turns per inch (TPI); 1 TPI = 39.37 TPM. Its direction is S or Z (section 01).

But 800 TPM means different things on different yarns. What the yarn feels is the angle its surface fibres make with its axis. For a yarn of diameter d with T turns per length, that angle obeys:

tan θ = π · d · T

A yarn’s diameter grows as the square root of its linear density. So a thicker yarn reaches the same angle with fewer turns: the same 800 TPM is a hard twist on a coarse yarn and a soft one on a fine yarn.

The twist multiplier folds the diameter in. It is turns scaled by the square root of the count, so equal multipliers mean equal surface angles at any count:

TM = TPI ÷ √Ne
The cotton-system twist multiplier (also called the twist factor). The usual figure on a spinning spec.
αtex = TPM × √tex
The metric twist factor. Because Ne × tex = 590.5, it is the same number in other units: αtex ≈ 957 × TM.

Twist is measured by untwisting a known length until the fibres lie parallel and counting the turns (ISO 2061, ASTM D1422).

What more twist does
Strength rises, then falls
A staple yarn holds together only by friction between its short fibres, and twist supplies that friction. An untwisted sliver has almost no strength, and strength climbs steeply as twist rises. Past an optimum the fibres lean further from the axis, so each carries less of the pull (the obliquity loss, roughly cos²θ), and strength falls again. A filament yarn’s fibres are continuous and carry load without friction, so twist adds only a little by making the filaments share load evenly. Its optimum is at low twist, and the obliquity loss starts almost at once.
Hand
Low twist is soft, lofty and full. High twist packs the fibres tight: lean, firm, crisp, sometimes harsh.
Luster
Flat, untwisted filament reflects most, because long parallel surfaces act as mirrors. Twist turns them away from the light and dulls it. On a staple yarn, moderate twist first tucks in the stray fibre ends and smooths the surface, then breaks it up at high twist.
Liveliness and snarling
Twist stores torque. A highly twisted single tries to untwist; let it go slack and it twists back on itself in a snarl. A lively yarn tangles in handling and can make a single-jersey fabric spiral (the wales twist on the bias).
Crepe
Very high twist (TM above about 5.5) made deliberately. When the fabric is wetted and relaxed in finishing, the yarns try to untwist and kink, and the fabric shrinks into a pebbled crepe surface.
Ply balancing
Plying two singles together with twist in the opposite direction (Z singles, S ply) cancels their torque. A balanced ply hangs in a loop without turning. That is the practical test: the ply twist that balances is found by trial, and it is usually less than the singles twist. A balanced yarn lies still, knits square and does not snarl. Covered yarn uses the same idea: the double covering in the next section wraps Z, then S.

Covered Yarn — Twist Direction

A covered yarn wraps a cover yarn around an elastic core. Single covered yarn (SCY) has one wrap, in either twist direction — S or Z, named for which way the diagonal of the letter runs. Double covered yarn (DCY) wraps twice: the inner layer goes on Z, then the outer layer wraps S over it, so the two torques cancel — the ply balancing of section 03, applied to a covered yarn.

Strength

“Strong” is several properties: how much force breaks the yarn, how far it stretches first, how stiff it is in the first few percent, and how much work it soaks up before it fails. They trade against each other, and the levers that raise one often lower another.

Some levers act on the polymer before there is a yarn, and some on the yarn itself. A test reports the yarn’s weakest place, not its average.

Four numbers from one test

A tensile test grips a fixed length of yarn and stretches it at a steady rate until it breaks, recording force against extension (ASTM D2256, ISO 2062). Four numbers come off the curve:

Tenacity
Breaking force divided by linear density: cN/dtex, cN/tex or gf/den. Dividing by the count is what lets a fine yarn and a coarse one be compared. Raw breaking force mostly says how thick the yarn is.
Elongation at break
How far it stretched before it broke, as a percentage of the gauge length.
Initial modulus
The slope at the start: resistance to the small stretches of everyday wear. It sets how firm or limp a yarn feels and how well a fabric keeps its shape.
Work of rupture
The area under the curve: the energy the yarn absorbs before it fails. Toughness, as distinct from strength. A yarn that stretches a long way at moderate force can absorb more than a stronger, stiffer one.

Rounded typical ranges; individual products sit anywhere in them or beyond. A spun yarn realises only about half its fibres’ tenacity, because short fibres hold together by friction and never all carry load at once: a ring-spun cotton yarn of 15–20 cN/tex from fibres of 30–40.

What changes it
Before there is a yarn — the polymer

Molecular weight. Longer chains entangle and share load over more of their length, so tenacity rises with chain length, up to the point where the melt is too viscous to spin. It is usually quoted as intrinsic viscosity: around 0.6–0.65 dL/g for textile polyester, 0.9 and above for high-tenacity industrial yarn.

Draw ratio and orientation. Stretching the freshly spun filament lines its molecules up along the axis. More draw gives more tenacity and modulus and less elongation; this is the POY → FDY step in section 01.

Crystallinity and heat setting. Ordered, crystalline regions carry load and resist heat. Heat setting anneals the structure so the yarn stops shrinking in later hot processing, usually for a small cost in peak tenacity.

In the yarn

Twist. For a staple yarn, strength rises with twist to an optimum and then falls (section 03). Filament yarns gain little.

Irregularity. A yarn breaks at its weakest place, so variation matters more than the average. The thin places of an uneven yarn set its strength, and its evenness (CV% of mass) is part of its strength spec.

Plying. A plied yarn’s weak places rarely line up, so it is more even and usually has a higher tenacity than its singles.

Filament count. At the same total denier, strength follows the polymer and the drawing, not the number of filaments. Finer filaments do make a softer yarn that abrades and pills sooner (a later section).

The weakest link

Pull a chain and it breaks at its weakest link. A yarn is the same: under test it fails at the weakest place within the gauge length. A longer specimen contains more places, and so is more likely to hold a weak one, so the same yarn reads weaker the longer the test length. F. T. Peirce worked this out for cotton yarns in 1926. It is why a test standard fixes the gauge length, and why two labs quoting the same yarn at different lengths disagree.
