Smart Textiles

A smart textile senses something, reacts to it, or both. Under every one of them is the same short chain: a yarn that conducts, a fabric that carries it, a mechanism that turns pressure, stretch, heat or a muscle’s signal into a change in resistance or charge, and a joint where the soft part meets the hard electronics.

Each diagram below runs on a loop and shows the mechanism at the scale where it happens — the fiber, the stitch, the electron. Petrol dots are current wherever it actually flows. Builds on Textile 101 and Conductive Trace Resistance.

What Makes It Smart

Xiaoming Tao sorts smart materials by how they react. Passive smart materials only sense the conditions around them. Active smart materials sense and react. Very smart materials sense, react, and adapt themselves to what they find.

The same cold morning runs through all three below: the air drops and recovers. The first garment reports it, the second switches on a heater at a fixed threshold, and the third changes its own rule to fit the person wearing it.

Making a Yarn Conduct

There are only a few ways to get a conductor into a yarn: coat an ordinary fiber with something that conducts, build the fiber from a conductive material, or combine a conductor with ordinary fibers by spinning, twisting, wrapping or braiding. Each one decides how far the current can travel, how much the yarn can stretch, and how long it survives washing.

Every diagram shows the process on the left and the yarn cut across on the right, with current only where the cross-section can carry it.

Into the Fabric

Once the yarn conducts, the fabric decides where the current can go. Woven and knitted fabrics build the conductor in as the cloth is made. Embroidery and couching add it afterwards, along any path. Printing puts it on top.

Sensing

Almost every textile sensor works the same way: something physical — pressure, stretch, temperature, a nearby muscle — changes a resistance, a capacitance or a voltage, and the electronics read that change. Each diagram shows the mechanism and, beside it, the reading it produces as it happens.

Acting

A textile that reacts needs an actuator: something that turns electrical energy into heat, color, movement or a change of shape. These are what move a garment from passive smart to active smart.

Antennas & Power

To be useful, a garment has to send what it senses and run on something. A conductive patch can be an antenna; and a few materials turn movement or body heat straight into electricity — though only in small amounts.

Soft Meets Hard

The batteries and chips are rigid and usually have to come off before washing. The joint between them and the fabric is where most smart textiles fail, so it gets its own engineering: connectors that snap, stick, attract or grip, and joints that are sewn and sealed for good.
