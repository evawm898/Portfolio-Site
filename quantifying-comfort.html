Quantifying Comfort

— How textile labs put numbers on a feeling. Part one is heat and moisture, from a Hohenstein talk; part two is touch.

01Three parts

“Comfortable” is subjective, so labs don’t measure it whole. Comfort has three components: thermal (heat and moisture), touch (how the fabric feels) and fit. This page covers the first two.

What sets them runs in a chain: fiber → yarn → fabric → finishing → garment and fit → layering.

Part one

Thermal

Heat and moisture: notes from a Hohenstein talk, each claim checked against its source.

02The system

Three parts: body, climate, clothing.

Heat gain is metabolic plus whatever the environment adds. It has to balance heat loss, which leaves five ways: conduction, convection, radiation, evaporation, respiration.

The controller is the hypothalamus. The effectors are the sweat glands, skeletal muscle and the skin arterioles.

03What gets measured

Two numbers: Rct, thermal resistance, in m²·K/W, and Ret, water vapor resistance — breathability — in m²·Pa/W.

Both come from the sweating guarded hotplate, the “skin model,” under ISO 11092.

05What I checked

  • Diffusion definition, thermoregulation, heat-balance model, ISO 11092 description: verified accurate.
  • Sweat table: in line with published sweat rates (often 0.5–2.0 L/h in exercise) and a measured marathon median of ~2.5 L (up to 6.8 L), but the marathon row runs high. Ironman 20 L would be gross production over 10–17 h, not net loss: finishers drink through the race, and the body-mass drop at the finish is far smaller. No primary source found for the table; treat it as illustrative.
  • Core-temp graphic claimed severe damage or death at ±4 °F. The hot side is close: heat stroke is above 104 °F. The cold side is overstated: 94.6 °F is mild hypothermia (below 95 °F); moderate starts below 89.6 °F and severe below 82.4 °F.

Part two

Touch

How the fabric feels in the hand.

06Hand

The industry word for touch is hand, or handle. “Soft” is several physical properties blurred into one word: a smooth, low-friction surface, low bending stiffness, and compressibility, the give under your fingers.

The reference method measures each one separately: the Kawabata Evaluation System for Fabrics (KES-F), a set of instruments for tensile and shear, bending, compression and surface. FAST (Fabric Assurance by Simple Testing, from CSIRO) is the cheaper industrial set. Instruments are calibrated against trained sensory panels: Kawabata’s hand scales were fitted to the judgments of expert assessors from the trade.

Japanese has words for the parts: koshi (stiffness), numeri (smoothness), fukurami (fullness).

Five tests follow. Every curve is illustrative: the shapes are right, the values aren’t measured. The instrument’s output is drawn in orange; switching fabric, or clicking a figure, reruns the test.

07Surface

Friction and roughness

Your hand
A fingertip slides across the cloth and feels drag and bumps.
The instrument
A probe on a light arm slides over the fabric at constant speed, logging friction and how far it rides up and down.

Friction is directional: the same fur reads low one way and high the other, which is why hand tests specify the direction of travel. KES takes three surface numbers from this pass: MIU, the mean coefficient of friction; MMD, its mean deviation, the graininess you feel; and SMD, geometric roughness from the probe’s up-and-down travel.

08Bending

Stiffness and drape

Your hand
You lift the fabric and feel how it falls over your fingers: crisp or limp.
The instrument
A strip slides off a flat edge until its tip drops to a line at 41.5°; the overhang at that moment is the reading (ASTM D1388, cantilever).

The stiffer the fabric, the further it sticks out before its own weight bends it down to the line. Half that overhang is the bending length, the standard’s measure of stiffness; the strip keeps sliding afterwards so you can see it drape. Drape testers go a step further: a circle of fabric hangs over a smaller disc, and the area of its shadow gives a drape coefficient.

09Stretch

Extension, power, recovery

Your hand
You pull the fabric and let go, feeling how hard it resists and whether it springs back.
The instrument
Two jaws pull a strip at a constant rate to a set load and back, logging load against extension.

The area between the loading and unloading curves is energy lost on the round trip; the gap where unloading meets zero load is growth, length the fabric keeps. ASTM D2594 covers stretch and growth of low-power knits like jersey; ASTM D4964 covers tension and elongation of elastic fabrics like power mesh.

10Squish

Compression, fullness, resilience

Your hand
You press the fabric between finger and thumb and feel how far it gives and how it pushes back.
The instrument
A platen presses down at a constant rate to a set pressure and lifts off, logging pressure against compression.

KES reads three numbers from this curve: LC, how linear it is; WC, the energy it takes to compress (more reads as fuller and softer); and RC, resilience, the share of that energy given back on release. The spacer plateau is published: warp-knitted spacer fabrics compress in three stages, linear elastic, plateau and densification, the plateau coming from the monofilaments buckling (Liu & Hu, 2016). Fleece just firms up steadily as its fibres pack.

11Warm or cool touch

Contact warmth

Your hand
You touch the fabric and, in the first moment, it feels warm or cool.
The instrument
A warmed plate is set down on the fabric and the peak heat flow out of it, q-max, is recorded (Kawabata’s Thermo Labo, KES-F7).

High q-max feels cool: heat leaves the skin fast. Low q-max feels warm. Brushed fleece touches the skin only at its fibre tips, with air between them; dense smooth nylon touches everywhere. It’s the same heat transfer as part one, felt in the first half second.
